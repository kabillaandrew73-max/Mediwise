Case 01

Case 01 — Fever and weakness

Presentation: A 20-year-old presents with fever, headache and marked weakness after several days of illness in a malaria-endemic setting.

Reasoning frame: Malaria, other infections and non-infectious causes can overlap clinically. Start with focused history, examination and appropriate testing rather than assuming the diagnosis from fever alone.

Think first

What additional history would you take? What examination findings matter? What test would help confirm malaria where available?

Learning point: The educational lesson is to separate the presenting syndrome from the final diagnosis and use appropriate testing.
Case 02

Case 02 — Repeated high blood pressure

Presentation: A 46-year-old has repeatedly elevated blood-pressure readings during separate visits but feels well.

Reasoning frame: Hypertension can be asymptomatic. Confirm measurement quality and the pattern of readings, then assess overall cardiovascular risk and possible secondary causes when indicated.

Think first

What measurement errors could falsely elevate BP? What other risk factors would you ask about?

Learning point: The key reasoning step is not to equate “no symptoms” with “no disease.”
Case 03

Case 03 — Wheeze and breathlessness

Presentation: A young adult reports episodic wheeze, chest tightness and shortness of breath, especially after triggers.

Reasoning frame: Asthma is one possibility, but several conditions can cause breathlessness or wheeze. History of triggers, variability, prior episodes and objective assessment are important.

Think first

What alternative diagnoses should be considered? What information would support variable airflow limitation?

Learning point: Clinical reasoning requires a differential diagnosis before settling on a label.
Case 04

Case 04 — Dysuria

Presentation: An adult reports burning urination and increased urinary frequency.

Reasoning frame: Lower urinary-tract infection is one possibility, but symptoms can overlap with other conditions. Consider pregnancy status where relevant, systemic symptoms, sexual history when appropriate and red flags.

Think first

Which findings would make this more urgent? What investigations might be appropriate?

Learning point: Good reasoning includes identifying the uncomplicated pattern and actively looking for features that change management.
Do not memorize the answer. For each case, practice a repeatable sequence: problem representation → differential → focused history/examination → appropriate investigations → management principles → red flags → follow-up.
